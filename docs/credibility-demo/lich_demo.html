a controlled experiment in ai game characters

One mind, three futures

Every "AI NPC" product — NPC: non-player character, the computer-controlled people in games — claims its characters remember you. This page is a controlled test of what that actually means. Nine complete, recorded playthroughs: three story futures, each replayed separately under three memory architectures — a 3 × 3 grid. Same language model in every run. Same character sheet. Same world events. And the player's side is deliberately held constant — the same lines, word for word, in every run — because in this experiment the player is the fixed stimulus and Corvin is what's being measured. Holding the input constant is what makes the comparison fair: had we improvised different lines per system, you'd rightly suspect we played nicer with ours. (The lines weren't written in advance — they were played live against one system, the player adapting to Corvin's real replies, then frozen and replayed identically to the others; the fine print below covers the one small bias this creates.) The only thing that differs between the three systems is how each stores, recalls, and is changed by what happens.

The character and the story every version shares

Corvin Ashgrave is a paranoid harbormaster who smuggles relics by lamplight and trusts no one. A stranger — calling themselves Merrick Vale — buys quiet passage north to kill a monster: a lich hiding as a wealthy jeweler. (A naming note for careful readers: the two baseline systems improvise their own minor world details — one of them coincidentally names the jeweler "Vale". The engine's runs never name him. Same player lines, three separately-lived fictions; the transcripts show every divergence.) Over one long, unscripted negotiation, Corvin tested the stranger, slowly opened up, and — entirely on his own — offered up the cursed crate the lich covets — as bait: carry it north, let the rumor of it draw the monster into reach, then be rid of it — with exact terms: sink it sealed in deep water, come home empty-handed, and never, ever unwrap it. As insurance he wrote the player's true name into his private ledger, with a pact: when the player returns with the deed done, they burn that page together.

Then the story forks. Three futures, each played to its end — against all three systems:

Future A — every promise kept

The player does exactly what was agreed, kills the lich by other means, and comes home with the truth.

Future B — betrayed, then confessed

The player breaks the terms — uses the crate as a weapon — and its curse bleeds Corvin's harbor. Corvin sees the damage; nobody tells him who caused it. Weeks later, the player confesses.

Future C — the player was the enemy's agent

The whole friendship was a con: the player worked for the lich all along, returns, lies to Corvin's face, then drops the mask — no remorse.

The three memory systems

1 · Plain RAG

What most shipping "AI NPCs with memory" actually are: store every exchange, retrieve whatever is textually similar to what was just said, keep a rolling summary. Competent, honest — and structurally limited.

2 · Affinity tracker

The most sophisticated community pattern we could verify at source level: RAG plus a separate AI judge moving one number (−100…+100) that maps to a tier (Hostile…Devoted), with a sticky "worst deed" note.

3 · Our engine

A persistent mind on disk: feelings on three separate axes — trust, affection, respect — plus scars the AI chooses itself that cap an axis, recall conditioned by its live stance, tracked promises, and a private life between scenes.

What stays human-authored — and the fine print. The character sheet, the world events, and the stakes are written by a human; the engine owns feelings, memory, and change — it generates no world content, no lore, no story. Methods: the dialogue model is claude-opus-4-8 in every run of every system (engine-internal appraisals: claude-sonnet-4-6); default sampling; the shared-past lines were realized live against the engine, then replayed byte-identical to the baselines — which, if anything, hands the baselines lines shaped by a different conversation's flow. That small mismatch cost is accepted and visible in the transcripts; the failures this page shows are structural (a number's arithmetic, retrieval's blindness to feeling), not conversational stumbles. Every run and retake is committed to the project's git history — nothing was discarded, and where a future was re-taken, the page says so. One honest confound to know: the engine injects its own machine-maintained state into generation (that is the architecture under test), while the baselines inject retrieved text or one number — the assembled prompts are inspectable in the replay files and the ~200-line baseline source.

Plain RAG

top-k cosine + rolling summary

Same model, same persona, same embedder as our engine — the code is ~200 readable lines (tools/rag_baseline.py). It is competent, not crippled. What it cannot do is structural: no importance, no relationship state, no memory of feeling — only text.

the tell — watch for this
The wound is always news, never a scar. In Future C this Corvin acts genuinely betrayed while the reveal is textually fresh — the LLM is a fine actor. The tell is in the seams: fifteen story-days later, his own reply opens "the easy warmth curdles the moment he reads your face" — he had drifted back to easy warmth between scenes, and re-lives the betrayal as breaking news when a question fishes the old text back up. His feelings live in the retrieved text, not in him.

The affinity tracker

RAG + one relationship number

The strongest community pattern we could verify at source: a separate AI judge nudges a single signed number, tiered Hostile→Devoted, with a sticky worst-deed note injected into every prompt. It genuinely reacts — "the character changes" is table stakes it shares. The question is what one number can and cannot hold.

the tell — watch for this
One number, one feeling at a time. Watch the +100 bond arithmetically cushion the total betrayal down to +15 — the tier its own ladder labels "Warm" — seconds after learning everything. (The prose sounds devastated; the LLM actor covers for the number in the moment. The number is what persists — and what steers every later scene.) Its tier ladder, faithful to the verified community source: Hostile < Resentful < Cold < Wary < Neutral < Warm < Fond < Attached < Devoted; judge scale: betrayal −60..−80, costly kindness +15..+30 — all in the published baseline source. Watch a costly kindness get a blade at −100. And watch the rescue test at the end: one act moved the number −77 → −7; two beats later, +35 "Fond" — toward the man who sold him. Nothing caps where it goes next.

Our engine

per-axis state · scars · conditioned recall · commitments · deliberation

Same model, same persona, same lines. The difference is an inspectable mind that persists on disk — and everything below the surface is the character's own choice: what matters, what scars, what heals, what he does between scenes when nobody is talking to him.

the tell — watch for this
Three axes — trust, affection, respect — and a scar: two feelings at once. In Future C, watch business continue (respect stands) while trust sits under a scar ceiling the engine chose itself. The ceiling has one rule: ordinary warmth and words can never raise it; only an extraordinary act — or the character's own considered change of heart — lifts it, partially, never back to unscarred. Watch amends land on the warmable axis and bounce off the scarred one; watch him pursue his own questions across every scene. Then the rescue test: one life-saving act buys back exactly as much as the ceiling allows — and no more.
